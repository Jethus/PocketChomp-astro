Calorie counting works the same way everywhere. Find roughly what you burn, eat a bit less or the same, log what you eat honestly, and adjust after a couple of weeks. What's different in Canada is everything around that: the food, the labels, the menus, the government guidance, and the fact that most calorie apps were built for someone else's grocery store.

This is the version of the process I'd give a friend in Canada who's starting today. It's practical, it's in metric where that helps, and it points out the Canadian-specific places where people's numbers go wrong.

## 1. Find your number first

Before you log a single meal, you need a target, and that starts with what your body burns in a normal day. It's called your TDEE, total daily energy expenditure, and the [TDEE calculator](/tools/tdee-calculator) estimates it from your height, weight, age, sex and activity. It gives you a range rather than one number, because the formulas are averages and real people sit on either side of them.

If you want to lose weight, the [calorie deficit calculator](/tools/calorie-deficit-calculator) takes that range and subtracts a pace you choose. For most adults the answer lands 300 to 500 calories below maintenance. [This post explains how to pick the pace](/blog/how-many-calories-to-lose-weight), and why 1,200 is a floor rather than a target.

One Canadian detail. If you've seen calories on a menu in Ontario, you'll have seen a line saying adults and youth 13 and older need an average of 2,000 calories a day and children 4 to 12 need 1,500, with a note that individual needs vary. That's a reasonable population average and a useful reference for reading a menu. It isn't your number. A tall, active person might maintain on 2,800, and a small, mostly seated one on 1,600.

## 2. Get a food scale and use grams

The single biggest accuracy upgrade in calorie counting is a kitchen scale. Volume measures are guesses. A "cup" of cereal can be 30 g or 50 g depending on how you pour, and peanut butter by the tablespoon is famously generous.

Canada makes this easier than it sounds, because every Canadian nutrition label has to give a metric amount for the serving. If the box says "Per 3/4 cup (30 g)" and you weigh out 45 g, you ate one and a half servings. No conversion tables, no ounces.

Most Canadians weigh themselves in pounds and their food in grams, or some mix of the two, and that's fine. Pick whatever units you think in for your body and use grams for the food.

You don't have to weigh everything forever. Weigh your common foods for a week or two, learn what your usual bowl or spoonful actually is, and you'll estimate far better after that.

## 3. Log groceries from the Canadian package

Packaged food is the easy part, as long as the data you're logging from is Canadian.

A lot of calorie apps were built on US data, and the entry you find for a Canadian product is often the American version of it. The name matches. The numbers might not, because recipes, serving sizes and fortification can differ between the two countries. You can usually tell from the entry itself: a % Daily Value beside total carbohydrate, an "Added Sugars" line, or servings in ounces with no metric amount all point to a US label. [This guide to reading Canadian nutrition labels](/blog/how-to-read-a-canadian-nutrition-label) goes through the label line by line, including those tells.

The fix is simple. Scan the barcode on the package in your hand. If the app doesn't know the product, or the entry looks American, enter the label from the box. It takes a minute and you only do it once per product.

For food without a label, like fruit, vegetables, meat from the butcher counter or bulk-bin lentils, use a generic entry. In Canada the best source for those is Health Canada's **Canadian Nutrient File**, a free database of nutrient values for thousands of foods commonly eaten here. Weigh it, then log the generic food by weight, raw or cooked to match the entry.

## 4. Eating out: use the calories that are posted

Restaurant meals are where logs get vague, and where Canada has a real advantage in one province.

**In Ontario,** food service chains with 20 or more locations in the province have had to post calories on their menus and menu boards since January 1, 2017. That covers fast food and coffee chains, but also places like grocery store hot counters and movie theatres that sell food made to eat right away. If you're in Ontario, the number is on the board. Log it as written.

**Everywhere else,** posted calories aren't required, but almost every large chain publishes a nutrition guide on its website. Use the Canadian site, not the US one. Menus, portion sizes and recipes for the same item can differ across the border, and searching "[chain] nutrition" will often land you on the American page first.

**At independent restaurants,** there's no number, so estimate by parts. Log the protein, the starch and the vegetables as separate items at a generous portion, then add a tablespoon or two of oil or butter for anything sautéed, fried or dressed. Restaurant cooking uses more fat than home cooking, and leaving it out is the most common way a restaurant meal is under-logged by several hundred calories. Being roughly right and consistent beats skipping the meal in your log.

## 5. Use Canada's Food Guide as the quality check

People are often surprised that Canada's Food Guide doesn't tell you how many calories to eat. The current guide, released in 2019, dropped the old serving counts entirely. What it gives you instead is a plate:

| Part of the plate | What goes there |
|---|---|
| Half | Vegetables and fruit |
| A quarter | Whole grain foods |
| A quarter | Protein foods, choosing plant-based ones more often |
| To drink | Water as the drink of choice |

That's actually a good partner for calorie counting, because the two answer different questions. Your calorie target answers "how much". The plate answers "of what". A day can hit its calorie number exactly and still be mostly crackers and cheese. The plate is the quick check that it isn't.

In practice, three things from the guide do most of the work when you're eating in a deficit:

- **Vegetables and fruit fill you up for few calories.** Half a plate of them makes a smaller calorie target much easier to live with.
- **Protein foods keep you full and protect muscle** while you lose weight. Beans, lentils and tofu count, and they bring fibre with them. The [macro calculator](/tools/macro-calculator) works out a protein target for your calorie goal.
- **Water instead of sugary drinks** is the easiest calorie cut there is, because drinks don't fill you up the way food does.

Fibre is worth tracking on its own here. Health Canada's adequate intake is 38 g a day for men and 25 g for women aged 19 to 50, and most Canadians get about half that. [This post has the reference numbers for fibre, protein and the rest by age and sex.](/blog/how-much-protein-carbs-fat-fibre)

## 6. Give it two weeks, then adjust

Your starting target is an estimate. The only way to find out whether it's right for you is to log honestly for about two weeks and watch your weight trend, not individual weigh-ins, which swing with water and salt.

- **Losing at about the pace you picked:** keep going.
- **Not losing after two full weeks:** first check the usual leaks. Cooking oil, sauces, drinks, tastes while cooking, and restaurant meals logged without the fat. If the log is honest, lower the target by 100 to 150 calories a day and give it another two weeks.
- **Losing much faster than planned and feeling flat:** eat a little more. Fast is not the goal. Still doing it in three months is the goal.

## The short version

1. Get a starting range from the [TDEE calculator](/tools/tdee-calculator) and pick a modest deficit.
2. Weigh your food in grams for the first couple of weeks.
3. Log packaged food from the Canadian package, not a US entry.
4. Use posted calories in Ontario and Canadian nutrition guides elsewhere. Add the oil at independent restaurants.
5. Use the Food Guide plate as a check on what you're eating, not how much.
6. Adjust after two weeks of honest logging.
