Food noise is the constant, unwanted thinking about food that has nothing to do with being hungry. Planning dinner while you're still eating lunch. The bag of chips in the cupboard that keeps announcing itself. The mental negotiation about whether you've "earned" dessert. It's the background hum, and for some people it's not background at all.

The term took off around 2022 and 2023, when people on semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy) started describing the hum going quiet. They weren't just less hungry. The thoughts stopped. That got researchers interested in something that didn't have a proper name before, and I've been asked a few times whether a tracking app can do anything about it, so here's what I found.

## It's not a diagnosis

Food noise isn't in any diagnostic manual. The first attempt at a scientific definition is a 2023 paper in *Nutrients* by Hayashi and colleagues, which frames it as a form of **food cue reactivity**: how strongly your brain responds to the sight, smell, thought or availability of food, and how much that response pushes you around. Their model has four parts. A cue (a smell, an ad, boredom, a time of day), things that influence how strongly you react to it (sleep, stress, dieting history, genetics, the drug you're on), the reaction itself (the thought, the craving, the pull), and the outcome (you eat, you don't, you spend twenty minutes arguing with yourself).

In 2025, a team at Pennington Biomedical published a validated **Food Noise Questionnaire**. It's five statements scored 0 to 4, for a total out of 20, things like "I find myself constantly thinking about food throughout the day" and "my thoughts about food feel uncontrollable." It's free for clinicians and researchers to use. The fact that a five-item scale holds up statistically is a sign that this is a real, measurable thing and not just a marketing phrase.

Two related ideas from older research help explain what's going on.

**Hedonic hunger.** In 2007, Lowe and Butryn proposed that a growing share of eating in rich countries is driven by pleasure rather than energy need, and that living surrounded by highly palatable food creates a persistent appetite that has nothing to do with being short on fuel. They also made a point that matters for anyone counting calories: the odd appetite behaviour you see in chronic dieters comes from eating less than they *want*, not less than they *need*.

**Elaborated intrusion.** A 2005 theory from Kavanagh, Andrade and May describes how a craving actually unfolds. A thought about food pops up on its own, triggered by a cue or a body state. If you engage with it, picturing the food, imagining the taste, you *elaborate* it, and that elaboration is what turns a passing thought into a craving. It also eats working memory, which is why food noise makes it hard to concentrate. The practical version: the thought isn't the problem, the dwelling is.

## What makes it louder

| Driver | Evidence | How strong |
|---|---|---|
| Restriction, skipped meals, big deficits | Minnesota experiment (1944), decades of dieting research | Strong |
| Sleep loss | fMRI study, one sleepless night | Moderate, small samples |
| Highly palatable food within reach | Cue reactivity research, observational | Consistent, mostly correlational |
| Stress | Cortisol and reward research | Consistent, hard to isolate |
| Forbidden-food rules | Restrained eating literature | Moderate |

The one I'd underline is **restriction**. During the Second World War, Ancel Keys put 36 healthy young men on roughly half their normal intake for six months, to learn how to refeed starving populations. Most lost more than a quarter of their body weight. What the men talked about afterwards wasn't the weight. It was the food obsession: collecting recipes, reading cookbooks, talking about nothing else, dreaming about it. Nobody dieting today is in that condition, but the direction holds all the way down. Eat meaningfully less than your body wants and food thoughts go up. That's not a willpower failure, it's the system working as designed.

**Sleep** is the second one worth knowing about. A 2013 study at Berkeley scanned 23 people after a normal night and after no sleep. One sleepless night turned down the frontal regions that weigh up food decisions and turned up the amygdala, and high-calorie foods became measurably more desirable. One night. Most people with persistent food noise are also chronically short on sleep, and it's rarely the first thing they look at.

## What turns it down

In order of strength:

**1. GLP-1 medication.** Nothing else comes close. A 2025 US survey of 550 people on semaglutide for weight loss found the share who said they thought about food constantly dropped from 62% before treatment to 16% on it. Median Food Noise Questionnaire score went from 13 out of 20 to 6. Two caveats. The survey was funded by the drug's manufacturer, and people were recalling how they felt before starting, which inflates the before number. But the effect is large, it matches what every clinician hears, and I'm not going to pretend any of the behavioural levers below is in the same league. If food noise is wrecking your life, that's a conversation with your doctor, not an app.

**2. Eat enough, and eat regularly.** This is the one most people get backwards. The instinct when food thoughts are loud is to clamp down harder, which makes them louder. Adequate protein and fibre, and not going too long between meals, is the single most reliable behavioural lever. A small 2013 trial had late-teen girls who normally skipped breakfast eat a 350-calorie breakfast with 35 g of protein, a 350-calorie cereal breakfast with 13 g, or nothing, each for six days. Only the high-protein breakfast reduced evening snacking on high-fat food. Both breakfasts lowered activity before dinner in the brain regions tied to food motivation, compared with skipping. Twenty people, about a week on each, so hold it loosely, but it points the same way as everything else.

**3. Change the environment, not yourself.** Out of sight is a real effect. Keep the stuff that calls to you out of the house, or out of view, or portioned at the store rather than at the counter. You're reducing the number of cues, which is cheaper than resisting each one.

**4. Make fewer food decisions.** Every "what should I eat" is an open question your brain can wander back into. Deciding the day in the morning, repeating meals that work, having a default lunch: each one closes a question before it can turn into noise. It's also the lever a tracker can actually help with.

**5. Un-forbid foods.** The restrained-eating research suggests that labelling food as off-limits increases how much you think about it. The evidence here is more mixed than the intuitive eating crowd makes it sound, but "nothing is banned, some things are planned" is a reasonable default for most people.

**6. Notice, don't elaborate.** From the intrusion theory: the thought arrives, you label it ("that's a food thought, I ate an hour ago"), and you don't follow it into the imagery. Ten minutes later it's usually gone. Small effect, zero cost.

**7. Sleep and stress.** Boring, and you already know. See the Berkeley study above for why it's not optional.

## What a tracking app can and can't do

I make a nutrition tracker, so here's the honest version.

A tracker **cannot** treat food noise. Anything that promised to would be overselling, and features like "log your cravings" or "resist the urge" push an app toward being a behaviour therapist, which it isn't qualified to be, and toward reinforcing the exact monitoring loop that makes some people worse. PocketChomp doesn't have those and won't.

What a tracker **can** do is avoid making it louder, and help with the one lever that's about decisions rather than willpower.

**Not making it louder** means the app shouldn't nag, shouldn't shame, and shouldn't push you to eat less than your body needs. In PocketChomp, protein and fibre are targets to *reach*, not ceilings to stay under. If seeing a calorie number at all is what feeds the noise for you, there's a toggle to hide calories entirely and track only the nutrients you care about. That toggle exists because of people close to me who needed it.

**Fewer decisions** is where tracking helps. If the same breakfast works, save it as a meal and log it in one tap instead of rebuilding it. Your frequent and recent foods sit at the top of Add Food instead of behind a search. And the diary lets you log tomorrow today, so if you know what dinner is going to be, you can put it in the night before and the question is already answered when 3 pm comes around. None of this is dramatic. The point is to take "what am I going to eat" off the table so there's less for the noise to grab onto.

It won't work for everyone. For some people the noise is mostly biology, and for them the drug is the answer and an app is bookkeeping. For a lot of people it's partly a restriction problem and partly a decisions problem, and both of those are fixable without a prescription.
