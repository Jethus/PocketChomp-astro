Looking for a free calorie counter in Canada is weirdly frustrating. You download an app, make an account, tap through twelve onboarding screens, and only *then* find out the barcode scanner wants a credit card or half your No Frills groceries aren't in there.

Almost every roundup you find on Google is written for the US and hasn't been updated properly since the big paywall changes. Half of them still call MyFitnessPal and Lose It! the best free apps, even though both now charge over CA$100 a year just to scan a barcode.

Full disclosure before you read further: I'm a solo dev in Toronto and I build [PocketChomp](/). I'll still tell you straight when one of the other apps is a better pick for you. Especially if you're on iPhone, because PocketChomp is Android-only right now.

## Why "free" apps feel worse in Canada than in the US

Even before paywalls, Canadian users get a rougher version of most calorie apps. You scan a box of cereal or yogurt and it pulls up the American entry: different recipe, ounces instead of grams, and macros that don't quite match the package in your hand.

Quick tell: if an entry in your app shows an "Added Sugars" line or a % Daily Value next to total carbs, it's an American label, not a Canadian one. (I broke down the exact differences in [how to read a Canadian nutrition label](/blog/how-to-read-a-canadian-nutrition-label).)

## If you're on iPhone

Since PocketChomp isn't on iOS yet, here's what I'd actually install on an iPhone today:

### FoodNoms
If you care about privacy and clean design, FoodNoms is awesome. It's an indie iOS app with no ads, free barcode scanning, free nutrition-label scanning, and iCloud sync instead of shipping your food log off to an ad network. It's honestly the closest cousin to what I'm trying to build on Android.

The main catch in Canada is the database leans American, so you'll end up using the label scanner more often on store brands like President's Choice or Compliments. And deeper micronutrients, AI logging and adaptive targets sit behind FoodNoms+ (US$39.99/yr).

### Cronometer
Cronometer is actually a Canadian company out of Revelstoke, BC, and they've stayed way fairer than the other big players. Barcode scanning is free, custom calorie and macro targets are free, and all 84 lab-verified nutrients (Health Canada CNF + NCCDB) are free.

The annoying parts on the free tier are the ads, needing a connection to search and log, and reports getting cut off after 7 days unless you pay CA$79.99/yr for Gold. Also, because they focus so much on whole-food lab data, random Canadian grocery house brands can be hit-or-miss. Full comparison here: [PocketChomp vs Cronometer](/vs/cronometer).

### MyNetDiary
If Cronometer feels a bit too clinical and you don't want ads, MyNetDiary is the other solid iPhone choice. Barcode scanning is free, the free tier has zero ads, and the database is cleaner than the messy crowdsourced entries in older apps. You still need an account, and deeper nutrient targets and photo scanning are paid.

## If you're on Android

### PocketChomp (my app)
I built PocketChomp mostly out of frustration. I wanted an app that actually had Canadian groceries from Loblaws, No Frills, Sobeys, Metro, Walmart and Costco, didn't charge $100+ a year to scan a barcode, and didn't force me to make an account just to log breakfast.

Everything core is free with zero ads: unlimited barcode scanning, on-device label scanning (works on bilingual English/French labels including commas like `0,5 g`), Health Canada's CNF plus Canadian store brands and [Canadian restaurant menus](/restaurants), macros in grams, all tracked micronutrients, CSV export, and Mindful mode if you want to hide calories completely and just look at protein or fibre. Your food log stays on your phone by default, works offline, and there's a [Data Receipt](/blog/what-leaves-your-phone) in settings that shows every request the app sends.

Being upfront about the downsides: it's Android-only for now, there's no web app, and the database is smaller than MyFitnessPal's if you buy a lot of niche imported stuff. PocketChomp+ is optional at CA$39.99/yr and covers things that cost money to run (photo/voice logging, URL recipe import) plus adaptive targets and trends past 30 days.

### FatSecret
Worth mentioning if you want something cross-platform that quietly never paywalled its barcode scanner. FatSecret has a decent Canadian food and restaurant catalogue and you can track calories and macros for free indefinitely. The UI feels dated and there are ads on the free tier, and you'll want to double-check community entries against the box the first time you log them.

## What about MacroFactor?

People bring up MacroFactor in literally every Reddit thread on this topic, so it feels weird to leave it off. **MacroFactor does not have a free version.** You get a 7-day trial, and after that it's CA$15.49/month, CA$59.99 for six months, or CA$90.99/year.

Honestly, I respect that a lot more than apps that advertise "free" and then nag you every three taps. There are no ads, the adaptive calorie algorithm is genuinely best-in-class, and the price is the real price. If you're happy paying for a tracker and want coaching-style weekly adjustments, MacroFactor is great. It just isn't free.

## Why I'd skip MyFitnessPal and Lose It! in 2026

Both apps still have huge databases and tons of smartwatch integrations. If you've been logging in one of them for years (or you're an older Lose It! user who still has grandfathered free barcode scanning), staying put makes total sense.

If you're starting fresh in Canada, though, there's not much reason to pick either for a free tier anymore. MyFitnessPal locked barcode scanning in 2022 and only gives free users percentage-based macro goals unless you pay CA$114.99/yr ([PocketChomp vs MyFitnessPal](/vs/myfitnesspal)). Lose It! moved barcode scanning behind CA$109.99/yr Premium for new accounts in 2026 with no monthly option. You can get all of that for $0 in the apps above.

## Do a 2-minute kitchen test before you commit

Whatever app you pick, don't spend an hour setting up meals on day one. Grab three things already sitting in your kitchen: one store-brand item (President's Choice, No Name, Compliments or Kirkland), one everyday staple like bread or yogurt, and one snack. Scan all three.

Did it ask for a credit card? Did the numbers match the Canadian Nutrition Facts table in grams? Did an ad pop up before you could finish logging? If an app annoys you during that two-minute test, you're going to hate it by week two.
