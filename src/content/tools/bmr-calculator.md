## What this calculator does

It runs the **Mifflin-St Jeor equation**, the formula most clinical and research settings
reach for first because it holds up better than the older Harris-Benedict across modern
populations:

- **Men:** (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) + 5
- **Women:** (10 × weight kg) + (6.25 × height cm) − (5 × age) − 161

That's the whole thing. Four inputs, one number. Worth understanding exactly because it
explains the limitation: the equation has no way of knowing whether you're carrying
significant muscle or very little, and lean mass is the single biggest driver of resting
burn. Two people matching on all four inputs can have genuinely different real rates.

## Why BMR is not your calorie target

This is the mistake worth avoiding. BMR is what you burn doing *nothing*. Your actual daily
burn — total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE — adds everything else:

- **Movement and exercise.** Anywhere from 15% to 30%+ of total burn.
- **The thermic effect of food.** Digestion itself costs roughly 10% of what you eat.
- **Non-exercise activity.** Fidgeting, standing, walking to the kitchen. Varies enormously
  between people — several hundred calories a day between a restless person and a still one.

Multiply BMR by an activity factor (1.2 sedentary through 1.9 for hard training plus a
physical job) and you get TDEE. **That** is the number intake decisions are made against.
Eating at BMR when your TDEE is 800 kcal higher isn't a careful deficit — it's a crash diet
you didn't intend, and it usually ends in a binge rather than a result.

## The honest limitation

Every BMR calculator on the internet, this one included, is a population-average prediction
dressed up as a personal number. Mifflin-St Jeor lands within about 10% for roughly four out
of five people — which still means a 1,600 kcal estimate could reasonably be 1,440 or 1,760,
and for one person in five it's further out than that.

So treat the output as a hypothesis, not a measurement. The way to find your real number is
to eat at a consistent, logged intake for two weeks and watch what your weight actually does.
The scale is measuring reality; the formula is guessing at it.
