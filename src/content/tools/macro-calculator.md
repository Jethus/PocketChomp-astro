## How this calculator works

Macros are a division of a calorie budget, so the calculation runs in that order:

1. **Estimate maintenance calories** from your stats using Mifflin-St Jeor plus an activity
   multiplier.
2. **Apply your goal** — a deficit for fat loss, a surplus for gaining, or maintenance.
3. **Set protein from bodyweight**, not from a percentage. Protein needs scale with the body
   you're feeding, which is why a fixed percentage misleads at the extremes of body size.
4. **Set fat as a share of calories**, keeping it above the level needed for hormonal health.
5. **Carbohydrate takes the remainder** — it's the most flexible macro and the one most
   worth adjusting around training.

## The five splits, and who each suits

**Balanced** — roughly a quarter to a third of calories from fat, protein set from
bodyweight, carbs filling the rest. The sensible default for most people, most of the time.
Enough carbohydrate to train properly, enough fat to feel satisfied.

**High protein** — raises protein to 2.0–2.6 g/kg and trims fat slightly. Worth choosing if
you're lifting seriously, in a meaningful deficit, or you simply find protein keeps you full.
The strongest evidence base of the five, particularly for holding lean mass while losing fat.

**Lower carb** — pushes fat to around 40% of calories and cuts carbohydrate accordingly. Not
ketogenic, just carb-reduced. Suits people whose appetite settles on higher fat, or who feel
better with steadier blood sugar. Expect a fast early weight drop that is water, not fat, and
expect high-intensity training to feel harder for a couple of weeks while you adapt.

**Low fat** — drops fat to about 20% of calories and lets carbohydrate take the room. Suits
high-volume endurance training, and anyone who simply eats better with more carbs on the
plate. Twenty percent is the floor here rather than a target to beat — fat below roughly
0.5 g/kg starts to interfere with hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption.

**Keto** — carbohydrate capped near 30 g a day, fat taking almost everything left, protein
held moderate rather than high (excess protein is gluconeogenic and works against ketosis).
Note that this is the one split defined by an absolute gram ceiling instead of a percentage,
because that is what actually determines whether you're in ketosis — a "5% of calories" rule
lands at 25 g on a small day and 44 g on a large one, which is only accidentally in range.
Worth a genuine caution: it's restrictive, it makes hard training feel worse for several
weeks, and at matched calories and protein it does not beat the others for fat loss.

## What actually matters, in order

Worth being blunt, because macro calculators tend to imply the split is the most important
decision you'll make. It isn't:

1. **Calorie total.** Decides the direction your weight moves. Nothing below this overrides it.
2. **Protein.** Decides how much of any loss comes from fat rather than muscle.
3. **Consistency.** A rough plan followed for months beats a precise one abandoned in three weeks.
4. **The carb-to-fat ratio.** Genuinely matters for how you feel and train — and barely at all
   for whether fat loss happens, at matched calories and protein.

If you take one thing from this page: get calories roughly right, hit protein most days, and
stop optimising the rest. The people who succeed at this aren't the ones with the cleverest
split.

## A note on precision

The grams here come from an estimated calorie target, which comes from an estimated
maintenance, which comes from a formula with a roughly 10% error bar. Stacking estimates
doesn't make them more exact — so treat these as a well-reasoned starting point and let two
weeks of real tracking tell you where they actually sit. Your weight trend is the only
measurement in this entire process; everything above it is a prediction.
